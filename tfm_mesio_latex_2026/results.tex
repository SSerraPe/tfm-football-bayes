\chapter{Results} \label{chap:results}

This chapter reports the results of fitting the model of Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology} to the production
La Liga player--season dataset described in Chapter~\ref{chap:data_source}. Unless stated otherwise, every
number, table, and figure in this chapter comes from the constant-$\sigma_e$, $K^*=3$, Student-$t$ production
fit (Definition~\ref{def:additive}, $\boldsymbol\Sigma_a$ as in Definition~\ref{def:sigma_a}) --- the model
whose specification, identification, and rank selection were argued for in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}. The
one deliberate exception is \S\ref{sec:results-phi}, which resolves the fixed-vs-estimated $\phi$ question
(Remark~\ref{rem:phi}) using a pair of separate fits built specifically for that comparison.

Convergence is excellent for the scalar and per-feature parameters: $\nu$, $\boldsymbol\psi_a$,
$\boldsymbol\sigma_b$, and $\boldsymbol\sigma_e$ all have $\hat R \leq 1.005$ and minimum bulk-ESS in the
hundreds to thousands. $\boldsymbol\Lambda_a$ itself mixes less cleanly (worst $\hat R = 1.014$, $4$ of the
$141$ sampled entries above the conventional $1.01$ threshold) --- but, as Remark~\ref{rem:ltpd_notinterp}
already anticipated, the raw loadings under the LT-PD identification constraint are an identification device,
not the object of substantive interest, so this is not by itself informative about whether the model has
recovered a genuine, stable shared-covariance structure. Proposition~\ref{prop:spectral_inv} gives the
rotation-invariant object that \emph{is} of substantive interest: the spectral decomposition of
$\hat{\boldsymbol\Lambda}_a\hat{\boldsymbol\Lambda}_a^\top$. Following that proposition, mixing was checked
directly on one representative entry of this rotation-invariant common-variance matrix, $[\boldsymbol\Lambda_a
\boldsymbol\Lambda_a^\top]_{1,10}$ (the goals--forward-passes pair, previously the single worst-mixing entry
identified during model development): $\hat{R} = 1.005$, bulk-ESS $\approx 237$, comfortably above the
$200$-draw threshold used throughout this chapter --- a clean pass, in contrast to the raw loadings'
noisier picture.

\section{Rank selection: results}
\label{sec:results-rank}

Two separate questions were posed in \S\ref{sec:rank}: whether low-rank $\boldsymbol\Sigma_a$ is worth having
at all ($K=0$ vs.\ $K\geq1$), and, if so, which rank to use ($K^*$ among $K\geq1$). The evidence for the two
questions is qualitatively different, and this section reports them separately rather than blending them.

\paragraph{$K=0$ vs.\ $K\geq1$: decisive.} PSIS-LOO on the rebuilt production dataset ($N=4{,}586$, $P=48$)
gives $\Delta\mathrm{ELPD} = 5{,}820.2$ (SE $343.5$, $\approx 17$ standard errors) favouring the low-rank
$K^*=3$ model over the fully diagonal $K=0$ baseline. Pareto-$\hat k$ exceeds $0.5$ for the large majority of
observations under both models ($98.9\%$ diagonal, $99.3\%$ low-rank), so this magnitude should be read
directionally rather than as a precise variance estimate --- but a nearly-$17$-SE gap is not the kind of result
a directional caveat can reverse. Shared player-level covariance structure is real and worth modelling.

\paragraph{Choosing $K^*$ among $K \geq 1$: qualitative, per \S\ref{sec:rank_qualitative}.} Table~\ref{tab:rank-criteria}
recaps the two structural criteria across $K=2,3,4$ that \S\ref{sec:rank_qualitative} defined; a player-holdout
cross-validation screen was also run across the same range and did not discriminate among these ranks either.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Criteria A and B across candidate ranks (\S\ref{sec:rank_qualitative}). Criterion A: last canonical
factor's share of $\bar{\boldsymbol\Lambda}_a\bar{\boldsymbol\Lambda}_a^\top$'s total variance. Criterion B:
mean fraction of standardised residual cells exceeding the fit's own $t(\hat\nu)$-implied $\tau_{99}$ threshold.
The final column reports each fit's own estimated Student-$t$ degrees of freedom $\hat\nu$, included for
reference rather than as a third criterion.}
\label{tab:rank-criteria}
\input{tables/43_rank_criteria_abc.tex}
\end{table}

Criterion A mildly favours stopping at $K=3$ (the fourth factor's $7.1\%$ share is smaller than the third's
$10.5\%$, but the decline from $K{=}2\to3\to4$ is continuous, not a sharp drop to a noise floor). Criterion B
is flat and uninformative at this range (all three ranks comfortably under the $1\%$ adequacy floor). Read
honestly, the case for $K=3$ over $K=4$ rests on Criterion A alone, and amounts to a preference for the simpler
model under a still-shrinking-but-not-negligible fourth factor, not a clean reversal of the kind that separates
$K=0$ from $K\geq1$. $K^*=3$ is retained on parsimony grounds and used throughout the rest of this chapter.

\section{Resolving the observation-level variance scaling ($\phi$)}
\label{sec:results-phi}

Remark~\ref{rem:phi} left open whether the minutes-exposure scaling exponent $\phi$ in
Definition~\ref{def:phi} should be fixed or estimated, deferring the decision to this chapter. The motivation
for scaling at all came from a direct diagnostic on the constant-$\sigma_e$ fit: of the top-200 worst
standardised-residual cells, $48.9\%$ ($88/180$) came from the bottom quintile of playing time, against an
expected $20\%$ under no minutes effect (binomial $p < 4.4\times10^{-18}$) --- observations from low-minute
player-seasons are markedly under-fit by a model that treats residual variance as constant regardless of
exposure.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Resolving $\phi$: constant-$\sigma_e$ baseline vs.\ two minutes-scaled variants. "Low-minute
worst-cell share" repeats the diagnostic above, recomputed on each fit's own residuals.}
\label{tab:phi-comparison}
\input{tables/43_phi_comparison.tex}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:phi-comparison} compares the constant-$\sigma_e$ production fit against two minutes-scaled
refits: $\phi$ fixed at the Poisson-rate value of $0.5$, and $\phi$ estimated freely. Both minutes-scaled
variants eliminate the low-minute over-representation almost entirely ($48.9\% \to 8.3\%$), confirming the
scaling addresses the diagnosed problem rather than merely reshuffling it. The estimated $\hat\phi = 0.366$
(90\% CI $[0.360, 0.373]$) is meaningfully below the fixed value of $0.5$: the data reject pure Poisson-rate
scaling in favour of a milder, super-Poisson exponent, consistent with some of a player's per-minute variance
being genuine heterogeneity (skill, role) rather than pure sampling noise that would vanish with more minutes.
Both scaled variants also push $\hat\nu$ higher (from $4.90$ to $5.67$--$5.98$): once exposure-driven
heteroscedasticity is accounted for, less of the remaining spread needs to be attributed to heavy tails.

\textbf{Verdict:} minutes-scaling with an estimated $\phi$ is the better-supported treatment for this data.
The rest of this chapter, however, continues to report results from the constant-$\sigma_e$ production fit
(stage 28): that is the fit all of the downstream infrastructure below (canonical loadings, archetypes,
similarity) was built against, and it is what Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology} describes as ``the model''
throughout. Re-deriving every subsequent result under the minutes-scaled variant is a natural extension, flagged
here rather than undertaken, since the qualitative substance of the loadings and similarity results is not
expected to change materially and the constant-$\sigma_e$ fit is the one already validated end-to-end.

\section{Variance decomposition: intraclass correlations}
\label{sec:results-icc}

Definition~\ref{def:icc} defines $\mathrm{ICC}_{a,p}$, $\mathrm{ICC}_{b,p}$, $\mathrm{ICC}_{e,p}$ as the share
of feature $p$'s total variance attributable to player, season, and residual, respectively.
Remark~\ref{rem:bayesian_icc} notes these are themselves posterior quantities, since every variance component
is sampled; Figure~\ref{fig:icc-top5} plots the posterior-median split for the five features with the highest
player ICC and, separately, the five features with the highest season ICC (the full 48-feature table is given
in Table~\ref{tab:icc-full}, Appendix~\ref{app:icc}).

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/45_icc_top5_stacked.png}
\caption{Player/season/residual variance share (posterior median) for the five features with the highest
player ICC (left) and the five features with the highest season ICC (right).}
\label{fig:icc-top5}
\end{figure}

Player identity dominates the left-panel features: all five exceed $\mathrm{ICC}_a = 0.85$ (recoveries,
offensive duels, touch in box, forward passes, crosses), with essentially no season component. The right
panel tells a complementary story: season ICC reaches $0.44$ for smart passes and $0.31$ for pressing duels,
features that do not otherwise stand out on the player-dominance ranking. This is the quantitative counterpart

substantially stable properties of the player for most features, but a handful of specifically tactical or
rule-sensitive features carry a real, non-trivial season signal --- \S\ref{sec:results-seasons} returns to
smart passes' season trend directly.

\section{Canonical factor loadings by feature group}
\label{sec:results-loadings}

Raw $\boldsymbol\Lambda_a$, fitted under the LT-PD constraint of \S\ref{sec:ltpd}, is an identification device
and should not be interpreted directly (Remark~\ref{rem:ltpd_notinterp}). Following
Definition~\ref{def:pca_postproc}, canonical factor directions are instead obtained by eigendecomposing the
common-variance matrix $\bar{\boldsymbol\Lambda}_a\bar{\boldsymbol\Lambda}_a^\top$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:spectral_inv} shows this is invariant to the identification constraint used at fit
time).

Three things stand out from Figure~\ref{fig:loading-heatmap}, one per axis.

\textbf{PC1} separates defensive volume from attacking output. Defending and passing-volume features load
positive --- recoveries, interceptions, clearances, forward and long passes --- while Attacking output loads
entirely negative: all seven of its features, from goals to touches in the box, carry a negative, reliable
loading. Passing itself splits by purpose rather than sitting on one side of the axis: bulk, directional
passing (forward passes, long passes, lateral passes) loads with the defensive pole, while creative passing
(key passes, smart passes, crosses, shot assists) loads with attacking output. This is the axis that separates
centre-backs from strikers most cleanly: Table~\ref{tab:archetype-combined}'s PC1 extremes are drawn entirely
from those two positions, with no overlap between them.

\textbf{PC2} separates technical, pass-based play from aerial and physical play. Passing volume, passing
quality, and progressive actions load entirely positive; the negative pole is anchored by aerial duels and
head shots, with clearances and blocked shots close behind and a mild negative loading on goals and expected
goals --- a target-man, aerial-duel profile rather than a purely defensive one. PC2 is largely independent of
PC1: it is why a target forward and a deep-lying passer can sit at a similar point on the defend/attack axis
while landing at opposite ends of this one, as Table~\ref{tab:archetype-combined}'s PC2 rows show directly
(Stuani, Budimir, Okazaki, and En-Nesyri at one extreme; Xavi, Iniesta, Rodríguez, and Kroos at the other).

\textbf{PC3} does not match a simple wide-versus-direct-play description, and is renamed accordingly. Its
positive pole combines possession quality --- the successful-pass rate is the single largest loading on the
axis --- with direct goal threat: head shots, goals, and expected goals per shot all load positively alongside
passing accuracy. Its negative pole is anchored by crossing, being dribbled past, and losing defensive duels, a
mix of wide attacking play and defensive engagement rather than either one cleanly. PC3 adds information the
other two axes do not carry: two attacking players who agree on PC1 and PC2 can still differ sharply in
whether their output comes through retained possession and clean finishing or through crossing into contested
situations, a distinction \S\ref{sec:results-similarity} returns to for two of its three case-study players.

\paragraph{Reconciling three factors with the earlier eleven.} Chapter~\ref{chap:background} reported an
eleven-factor exploratory solution from classical factor analysis of the raw, unpartitioned player--season
matrix. Remark~\ref{rem:k_not_comparable} explains why $K^*=3$ is not in tension with that finding: the
classical analysis describes the total covariance of $\mathbf y_n$, in which any two features that co-vary for
\emph{any} reason --- shared player skill, a league-wide tactical shift, or coincidental season drift ---
contribute jointly to the recovered factor count. $\boldsymbol\Sigma_a$, by contrast, is the covariance of what
is left of a player \emph{after} season-level drift and observation noise are subtracted out
(Definition~\ref{def:additive}); a smaller $K$ for that narrower object is the expected consequence of
conditioning on more structure, not a contradiction between the two analyses. As that remark itself notes, this
explanation has not been independently validated (e.g.\ by refitting the classical FA on a player-only,
season-averaged version of the data) and should be read as an argument for the direction of the discrepancy,
not a demonstrated equivalence.

\section{The canonical style space}
\label{sec:results-stylespace}

Projecting each player's posterior-mean effect onto the three canonical directions gives every player a
position in a $3$-dimensional style space. $k$-means clustering ($k=5$) on the (PC1, PC2) plane recovers five
groups that align closely with recognisable positional roles once ordered by PC1: Forwards, Attacking
midfielders, Midfielders, Defensive midfielders, and Centre backs, running from the most attacking to the most
defensive end of PC1. This structure is not replotted below, since Figure~\ref{fig:style-space-scatter} uses
colour to track individual players across three specific projections rather than five population clusters, but
it stands as independent confirmation that the space this chapter works with corresponds to real footballing
structure, not an artefact of the model.

--- consistent with the broader decline visible in the Passing volume and Passing quality panels of
Figure~\ref{fig:season-trends}.

\section{Player similarity: separating style from level}
\label{sec:results-similarity}

This section takes up, in depth, the question the Introduction posed at the outset: what does it mean for two
players to be ``similar,'' and can a player be stylistically similar to an outlier without matching their
output?

\subsection{Motivation: separating style from level}

The model's own covariance structure already gives a principled way to make this split. Recall
(Definition~\ref{def:sigma_a}) that the player effect decomposes as
\[
\mathbf a_i = \boldsymbol\Lambda_a\boldsymbol\eta_i + \boldsymbol\psi_a \odot \mathbf z_{u,i},
\]
a \emph{shared} component driven by the $K^*=3$ canonical factors, and an \emph{idiosyncratic} component that
those factors do not explain. \textbf{Style} is naturally read off the shared component: a player's position in
the $3$-dimensional canonical factor space, $\mathbf f_i$ (Definition~\ref{def:pca_postproc}), the same
coordinates used for the archetype clustering of \S\ref{sec:results-stylespace}. \textbf{Level} is naturally
read off the specific output features that shared style position does not by itself pin down --- here, goals
and expected goals per shot. Both are evaluated at their posterior mean throughout this section.

Two players can therefore be \emph{style-close} (near-identical $\mathbf f_i$) while being \emph{level-far}
(very different modelled goal output). That is the model-native version of ``can a player be stylistically
similar to an outlier without matching their production'': yes, and the mechanism is explicit rather than
asserted. It also explains why a true generational outlier is unlikely to have a perfect twin --- their
outlier-ness lives disproportionately in $\boldsymbol\psi_a \odot \mathbf z_{u,i}$, the part of their identity
that is, by construction, not shared with anyone else. Matching someone's shared style is achievable; matching
both their style and their most idiosyncratic output is structurally much less likely.

\emph{A note on scope}: the dataset is La Liga only, 2011/12--2022/23 (Chapter~\ref{chap:data_source}). A player like
Erling Haaland, who has never played in this league, is not in this analysis. He is a useful illustration of
the ``generational outlier'' idea in the abstract, never a literal query target here --- every concrete example
below uses an actual in-sample player as the stand-in.

\subsection{Case studies: three targets}

Style distance between two players is the Euclidean distance between their posterior-mean canonical factor
scores $\bar{\mathbf f}_i$, standardised across all 1{,}529 outfield players; level gap is the Euclidean
distance between their posterior-mean per90 goals and expected-goals effects. Both are single numbers per
pair, reported directly as distances rather than converted to a bounded similarity score: smaller means more
similar, and the underlying quantity is already a Euclidean distance in standardised canonical-score space,
the same one used throughout this chapter. Introducing a monotone transform to a $[0,1]$ scale here would add a
mathematical object the thesis does not otherwise need.

The remainder of this section works through three named players: Messi, Sergio Ramos, and Xavi. The primary
reason for this choice is that a reader already has an informed opinion about how each of these three plays,
which is what makes the model's output checkable rather than merely plausible --- an unfamiliar player's
neighbour list cannot be judged against prior knowledge, a familiar one's can. A secondary reason is that their
canonical scores span real extremes of the space, though less completely than a one-player-per-axis account
would suggest. Messi is an extreme on both PC1 (4th most extreme of 1{,}529 players) and PC3 (the single most
extreme value in the dataset). Xavi is an extreme on PC2 (also the single most extreme value) and PC3 (2nd most
extreme), but sits close to the population centre on PC1 (1{,}123rd by magnitude, near the median). Ramos is a
clear PC1 extreme (26th most extreme, the positive pole) but not a PC2 extreme (1{,}451st, essentially the
population median) and shares PC3's negative pole with Messi and Xavi rather than anchoring an opposing one.
Between the three, both poles of PC1 are covered and the shared negative pole of PC2 and PC3 is covered twice
over; PC2's and PC3's positive poles are not represented by any of the three. This is a genuine partial
coverage of the space, not a tidy one-axis-each account, and is reported as such.

sit clearly apart on every axis.

\paragraph{Ramos.} Javi García ($0.161$) and Gerard Piqué ($0.164$) sit within $0.003$ of each other in style
distance from Ramos, both clearly ahead of the third-closest, Samuel Umtiti ($0.316$). Figure~\ref{fig:score-forest}
shows what the point estimates alone do not: on PC1, Ramos's own credible interval overlaps all three
neighbours, and García's and Piqué's overlap each other almost entirely. The near-tie in Table~\ref{tab:similarity}
is a real near-tie in the posterior, not an artefact of reporting three decimal places. Piqué's level gap to
Ramos ($0.77$) is smaller than García's ($1.13$), which may explain why the well-known Ramos--Piqué pairing
persists in football conversation even though García edges ahead on style alone. One of Ramos's three most
distant players is Messi himself --- the same PC1 contrast this chapter returns to repeatedly, restated here
as a distance rather than a pair of separate extremes.

\paragraph{Xavi.} Miralem Pjanić ($0.65$), Toni Kroos ($1.19$), and Andrés Iniesta ($1.29$) are three players
built around retaining and circulating possession, matching PC2's description of Xavi's game directly, and all
three level gaps are small (Pjanić's, $0.32$, is the smallest anywhere in Table~\ref{tab:similarity}). Xavi's
three most distant players --- Budimir, Stuani, and En-Nesyri --- are all aerial-target forwards, PC2's
opposite pole. Figure~\ref{fig:score-forest}'s PC2 panel makes the contrast visually explicit: Xavi's cluster
and the three forwards' do not overlap at all.

\section{Applications: scouting and recommendations}
\label{sec:results-scouting}

The style/level decomposition of \S\ref{sec:results-similarity} turns directly into a scouting-relevant product:
a shortlist of players who match a target's style at a different output tier, rather than an attempted
like-for-like replacement of an irreplaceable talent. Concretely, the recipe is: fix a target player, take their
top style-neighbours (as in Table~\ref{tab:similarity}), and read off which of those neighbours
has the largest \emph{level gap} --- that neighbour is a plausible ``cheaper alternative'' candidate, in the
sense of matching shape while differing in the specific output dimension a club may be more willing to accept a
discount on.

This is a style/production tool, not a recruitment decision system, and should be read as one input among many:
market value, age, injury history, contract situation, and league context are not modelled anywhere in this
pipeline. Within those limits, it offers something a purely descriptive scouting report cannot: an explicit
separation of ``how a player plays'' from ``how much they currently produce.''

\section{Future work}
\label{sec:results-future}

Three directions were considered during this work and are reported here as scoped future work rather than as
contributions of the present thesis.

\textbf{Uncertainty beyond posterior means.} The model itself is fully Bayesian throughout: every variance
component, loading, and rank-selection criterion in Chapters~\ref{chap:methodology} and~\ref{chap:results}
carries a posterior distribution, not a point estimate, and individual canonical scores are no longer an
exception --- Figure~\ref{fig:score-forest} reports a 90\% credible interval for each case-study player on
every axis, under the fixed reference orientation of Remark~\ref{rem:posterior_mean_anchor}. Two other
summaries still reduce the posterior to a single number. The style-space clustering of
\S\ref{sec:results-stylespace} assigns each player to a single $k$-means cluster on $\bar{\mathbf f}_i$, and the
similarity table of \S\ref{sec:results-similarity} reports one style distance and one level gap per pair rather
than a credible interval on either. A soft, probability-weighted archetype membership per player (e.g.\ from a
Gaussian mixture over the canonical factor scores, letting a player be reported as, say, 70\% centre-back and
30\% defensive midfielder) is a direct extension of the scores now being reported with uncertainty, and remains
open. A credible interval on a \emph{pairwise distance} is a different, harder problem: it depends on the joint
draw-by-draw behaviour of two players' scores together, not on either player's marginal interval alone, and
was not attempted here.

\textbf{Distance-based latent-space models.} The factor-analytic route taken in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}
represents player style through a shared covariance structure. A complementary approach, not pursued here,
represents player style directly through pairwise dissimilarities via Bayesian multidimensional scaling
\cite{oh2001bayesian}, with hierarchical extensions available for repeated player--season observations
\cite{yanchenko2020hierarchical}. The classical multidimensional scaling analysis of
Chapter~\ref{chap:background} is a first, non-probabilistic step in that direction; a fully Bayesian version
would attach posterior uncertainty directly to each player's position in the similarity space, which the
present factor-analytic model instead obtains indirectly, through the canonical factor scores.

\textbf{Team-season effects and tactical fit.} No team identifier exists anywhere in the current data pipeline
(confirmed absent from both the raw SQL extraction and the model's row-mapping table), so a genuine
player-vs-team-system comparison is not buildable without new data engineering back to the Wyscout source.
Adding a team-season effect $c_{t(i,j),j}$ would separate manager and tactical-system effects from stable
player identity, and would be the natural foundation for a real tactical-fit score; absent that extension, the
closest available proxy is simply which of the five archetype clusters (\S\ref{sec:results-stylespace}) a
player belongs to.
