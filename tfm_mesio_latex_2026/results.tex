\chapter{Results} \label{chap:results}

This chapter reports the results of fitting the model of Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology} to the production
La Liga player--season dataset described in Chapter~\ref{chap:data_source}. Unless stated otherwise, every
number, table, and figure in this chapter comes from the minutes-scaled, fixed-$\phi=1/2$, Student-$t$
production fit at $K^*=3$ (Definition~\ref{def:additive}, $\boldsymbol\Sigma_a$ as in
Definition~\ref{def:sigma_a}, residual scale as in Definition~\ref{def:phi}), the model whose
specification, identification, and rank selection were argued for in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}.
\S\ref{sec:results-rank} confirms $K^*=3$ directly on this family, alongside $K=2$; \S\ref{sec:results-phi}
reports the diagnostic comparison against an earlier constant-residual-scale fit and against a fit with $\phi$
estimated rather than fixed, which is why numbers from those two fits appear there and only there.

\section{Model diagnostics and convergence}
\label{sec:results-diagnostics}

Table~\ref{tab:diagnostics-summary} summarises posterior convergence for the production fit, grouped by
parameter family. $\hat R$ and effective sample size are computed in the usual way from four parallel chains.
Three of the $144$ entries of $\boldsymbol\Lambda_a$ are excluded from the $\boldsymbol\Lambda_a$ row: under the
lower-triangular, positive-diagonal constraint of \S\ref{sec:ltpd} these entries are fixed at exactly zero and
never sampled, so $\hat R$ is undefined for them (zero posterior variance) rather than poorly mixed.

are the season and residual scales. The $\boldsymbol\Lambda_a\boldsymbol\Lambda_a^\top$ row summarises all

Convergence is excellent for the scalar and per-feature parameters: $\nu$, $\boldsymbol\psi_a$,
$\boldsymbol\sigma_b$, and $\boldsymbol\sigma_e$ all have $\hat R \leq 1.005$ and minimum bulk-ESS in the
hundreds to thousands. $\boldsymbol\Lambda_a$ itself mixes markedly less cleanly (worst $\hat R = 1.032$,
$36$ of the $141$ free entries above the conventional $1.01$ threshold), an identification artefact
anticipated by Remark~\ref{rem:ltpd_notinterp}: the raw loadings under the LT-PD constraint are not
themselves the object of substantive interest, so this is not by itself informative about whether the model
has recovered a genuine, stable shared-covariance structure.

Proposition~\ref{prop:spectral_inv} gives the rotation-invariant object that \emph{is} of substantive
interest: the common-variance matrix $\hat{\boldsymbol\Lambda}_a\hat{\boldsymbol\Lambda}_a^\top$. Rather than
checking one representative entry, Table~\ref{tab:diagnostics-summary} reports convergence over all $1{,}176$
of its unique entries ($48$ diagonal, $1{,}128$ off-diagonal): median (minimum) bulk-ESS $603$
($154$), maximum $\hat R = 1.025$, with $12$ of $1{,}176$ entries ($1.0\%$) above $1.01$. That contrast is
the point: the raw loadings look markedly worse than they did under the constant-scale fit, while the
rotation-invariant object built from them looks, if anything, cleaner. Two things are worth saying about
that last count, though. With $1{,}176$ separate checks, a handful above the conventional $1.01$ threshold
is expected from multiple comparisons alone and is not, by itself, a sign of a mixing problem. $\hat R$ is
also a less reliable diagnostic for entries whose posterior is concentrated near zero, which describes many
off-diagonal entries at rank $3$; restricting to entries with posterior-mean magnitude above $0.05$ changes
the picture only slightly, to $11$ of $1{,}071$ entries above $1.01$, maximum $\hat R = 1.025$, so this
caution does not materially change the read here. The second row of the table gives the same summary for
the three eigenvalues $d_1,d_2,d_3$ of $\hat{\boldsymbol\Lambda}_a\hat{\boldsymbol\Lambda}_a^\top$ directly:
bulk-ESS from $216$ to $551$, $\hat R \leq 1.004$ for all three, the cleanest read of convergence for the
quantities Criterion A and the variance-share table below are actually built from.

\section{Resolving the observation-level variance scaling ($\phi$)}
\label{sec:results-phi}

Remark~\ref{rem:phi} states that the production model fixes $\phi=1/2$. This section is where that choice is
argued for, against a diagnostic fit with $\phi$ estimated and against the constant-scale specification both
were built to correct. The motivation for scaling at all came from a direct diagnostic on the constant-scale
fit: of the top-200 worst standardised-residual cells, $48.9\%$ ($88/180$) came from the bottom quintile of
playing time. An even split by minutes would predict $20\%$; the gap is not explainable by chance (binomial
$p < 4.4\times10^{-18}$). Observations from low-minute player-seasons are markedly under-fit by a model that
treats residual variance as constant regardless of exposure.

\begin{table}[tbp]
\centering
\caption{Resolving $\phi$: the constant-scale specification vs.\ two minutes-scaled variants. "Low-minute
worst-cell share" repeats the diagnostic above, recomputed on each fit's own residuals.}
\label{tab:phi-comparison}
\input{tables/43_phi_comparison.tex}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:phi-comparison} compares the constant-scale fit against two minutes-scaled refits: $\phi$ fixed
at the Poisson-rate value of $0.5$, and $\phi$ estimated freely. Both minutes-scaled variants eliminate the
low-minute over-representation almost entirely, from $48.9\%$ to $8.3\%$. Scaling addresses the diagnosed
problem; it does not just reshuffle it. The estimated $\hat\phi = 0.366$ (90\% CI $[0.360, 0.373]$) sits below
$0.5$. Residual noise therefore falls off more slowly with playing time than pure sampling error would
predict, which is what you would expect if part of the extra variation in low-minute seasons is a genuine
difference between players rather than measurement noise. Both scaled variants also push $\hat\nu$ higher,
from $4.90$ to $5.67$--$5.98$: once exposure-driven heteroscedasticity is accounted for, less of the
remaining spread needs to be attributed to heavy tails.

\textbf{Verdict:} minutes-scaling is necessary; the remaining question is whether $\phi$ should be fixed or
estimated, and the production model fixes it at $0.5$. It is the value predicted directly by Poisson-rate sampling theory, not a value read off this
particular fit, so it does not risk fitting noise in the exponent itself. It also introduces one fewer free
parameter than treating $\phi$ as unknown, at a cost the estimated fit's own posterior suggests is small.
Every subsequent result in this chapter comes from the minutes-scaled, fixed-$\phi=0.5$ fit.

\section{Rank selection: results}
\label{sec:results-rank}

Two separate questions were posed in \S\ref{sec:rank}: whether low-rank $\boldsymbol\Sigma_a$ is worth having
at all ($K=0$ vs.\ $K\geq1$), and, if so, which rank to use ($K^*$ among $K\geq1$). The evidence for the two
questions is qualitatively different, and this section reports them separately rather than blending them. Both
are now evaluated on the production family fixed in \S\ref{sec:results-phi}.

\paragraph{$K=0$ vs.\ $K\geq1$: decisive.} The left panel of Figure~\ref{fig:ppc-correlation} shows what a
diagonal player covariance implies: every model-implied cross-feature correlation is exactly zero, by
construction ($\boldsymbol\Lambda_a$ has no columns, and $\boldsymbol\Sigma_b$, $\boldsymbol\Sigma_e$
contribute nothing off-diagonal either). Observed correlations span roughly $-0.7$ to $+0.9$ across the same
$1{,}128$ feature pairs. A model with $K=0$ cannot reproduce this range under any parameter values; the right
panel shows a $K\geq1$ model can, closely. This is not a directional or approximate comparison: it follows
from the structure of the $K=0$ model itself, not from any diagnostic that could be read as inconclusive.
Shared player-level covariance structure is real and worth modelling.

\paragraph{Choosing $K^*$ among $K \geq 1$: qualitative, per \S\ref{sec:rank_qualitative}.} Table~\ref{tab:rank-criteria}
recaps the two structural criteria across $K=2,3,4$ that \S\ref{sec:rank_qualitative} defined. $K=2$ and $K=3$
are evaluated on the minutes-scaled, fixed-$\phi=1/2$ family fixed above; $K=4$
is not refit under that family (the existing constant-scale $K=4$ fit is orders of magnitude more expensive to
sample than $K=2$ or $K=3$, for reasons unrelated to minutes-scaling), so its row is carried over from the
constant-scale family and reported as such rather than silently mixed in.

\begin{table}[tbp]
\centering
\caption{Criteria A and B across candidate ranks (\S\ref{sec:rank_qualitative}). Criterion A: last canonical
factor's share of $\bar{\boldsymbol\Lambda}_a\bar{\boldsymbol\Lambda}_a^\top$'s total variance. Criterion B:
mean fraction of standardised residual cells exceeding $\tau_{99}$, the $99$th percentile of each fit's own
$t(\hat\nu)$ (each fit's estimated degrees of freedom $\hat\nu$ therefore differs, but is not shown as a
separate column here). $K=4$'s row is from the constant-residual-scale family (see text); $K=2$ and $K=3$ are
from the production, minutes-scaled family.}
\label{tab:rank-criteria}
\input{tables/43_rank_criteria_abc.tex}
\end{table}

Criterion A mildly favours stopping at $K=3$ (the fourth factor's $7.1\%$ share is smaller than the third's
$10.4\%$, but the decline from $K{=}2\to3\to4$ is continuous, not a sharp drop to a noise floor). Criterion B
is flat and uninformative at this range (all three ranks comfortably under the $1\%$ adequacy floor). Read
honestly, the case for $K=3$ over $K=4$ rests on Criterion A alone, and amounts to a preference for the simpler
model under a still-shrinking-but-not-negligible fourth factor, not a clean reversal of the kind that separates
$K=0$ from $K\geq1$. $K^*=3$ is retained on parsimony grounds and used throughout the rest of this chapter.

\section{Variance decomposition: intraclass correlations}
\label{sec:results-icc}

Definition~\ref{def:icc} defines $\mathrm{ICC}_{a,p}$, $\mathrm{ICC}_{b,p}$, $\mathrm{ICC}_{e,p}$ as the share
of feature $p$'s total variance attributable to player, season, and residual, respectively, evaluated at the
reference exposure $m_n=m_{\mathrm{ref}}$: under the production model's minutes-weighted residual scale the
residual variance depends on playing time, so this is the variance decomposition for a player--season
observation at the reference minutes figure, not an average over observations.
Remark~\ref{rem:bayesian_icc} notes these are themselves posterior quantities, since every variance component
is sampled; Figure~\ref{fig:icc-top5} plots the posterior-median split for the five features with the highest
player ICC and, separately, the five features with the highest season ICC (the full 48-feature table is given
in Table~\ref{tab:icc-full}, Appendix~\ref{app:icc}).

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/45_icc_top5_stacked.png}
\caption{Player/season/residual variance share (posterior median) for the five features with the highest
player ICC (left) and the five features with the highest season ICC (right).}
\label{fig:icc-top5}
\end{figure}

Player identity dominates the left-panel features: all five exceed $\mathrm{ICC}_a = 0.85$ (recoveries,
touch in box, forward passes, offensive duels, crosses), with essentially no season component. The right
panel tells a complementary story: season ICC reaches $0.44$ for smart passes and $0.30$ for pressing duels,
features that do not otherwise stand out on the player-dominance ranking. This is the quantitative counterpart
to the qualitative claim motivating the whole model (\S\ref{sec:covariance}): football performance profiles are
substantially stable properties of the player for most features, but a handful of specifically tactical or
rule-sensitive features carry a real, non-trivial season signal. \S\ref{sec:results-seasons} returns to
smart passes' season trend directly.

This split is only available because the model gives player and season effects their own covariance
structures from the outset, rather than fitting a single factor matrix to the pooled observations the way a
classical, frequentist factor analysis does (Chapter~\ref{chap:background}). A one-shot analysis of the raw
player--season rows has no player or season index to condition on, so any covariance between two features,
whether it comes from stable player skill or a league-wide shift concentrated in a few seasons, is folded into
the same estimated structure with no way to separate the two afterward. Here the low-rank factor structure of
Definition~\ref{def:sigma_a} applies specifically to $\boldsymbol\Sigma_a$, so the ICC decomposition above
falls directly out of that separation, not out of anything recoverable from a single fitted covariance matrix.

\section{Season profiles}
\label{sec:results-seasons}

Consistent with Figure~\ref{fig:icc-top5}, season ICC is small for almost every feature, but small does not
mean zero, and group-averaged season effects reveal coherent league-wide drift over the twelve seasons in the
data (Figure~\ref{fig:season-trends}).

% the "Chance creation" paragraph that discusses it -- plain floating let it drift past that
% discussion to the bottom of the page instead.
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{images/33_season_group_trends.png}
\caption{Group-averaged posterior-mean season effects $\hat{\mathbf b}_j$, in Z-scaled feature units, one panel
per feature group, 2011/12--2022/23. Most groups drift downward from around 2017/18 onward.}
\label{fig:season-trends}
\end{figure}

Chance creation---the creative, line-breaking side of the passing game (smart passes, key passes, crosses,
shot assists, through balls; see Section~\ref{sec:results-loadings})---shows the cleanest and largest swing of
any group in Figure~\ref{fig:season-trends}: a high in exactly 2011/12--2012/13, the seasons spanning the end
of Pep Guardiola's Barcelona and Tito Vilanova's title-winning continuation of it, followed by an almost
uninterrupted decline for the rest of the sample. Dueling follows a related but less clean pattern: elevated in
the same two seasons, it dips through the mid-2010s and recovers to a second, comparable high around 2017/18
before falling away sharply. Passing volume---bulk, directional ball circulation rather than creative
passing---tells a different story: it is at its lowest, not its highest, in 2011/12, and only climbs back to
its sample peak at the very end of the window (2022/23). Read together, the split argues that what was
actually elevated in the Guardiola/Vilanova era was purposeful, creative passing specifically, not passing
volume in general, which is exactly the distinction the group split in Section~\ref{sec:results-loadings} was
designed to expose. Passing quality and Progressive actions move less cleanly season to season but both sit
within a broadly elevated regime through the mid-2010s, a period that includes Luis Enrique's Barcelona
(2014/15--2016/17): still dominant, but built more around the pace and individual quality of Messi, Suárez, and
Neymar in transition than Guardiola's extended possession sequences, a real, partial step away from pure
tiki-taka before the league-wide decline properly sets in. Figure~\ref{fig:season-trends} is a league-wide
average across every club, not Barcelona's own numbers, so no single team's history explains it on its own; but
the timing matches the qualitative story football writers were already telling about this period, and the
decline visible in most panels from around 2017/18 onward is consistent with a broader shift toward faster,
more direct, transition-oriented football across La Liga later in the decade.

\begin{figure}[tbp]

\caption{The six features with the largest peak-to-trough range in $\hat{\mathbf b}_j$ across seasons. Smart

Smart passes stands out not just for its range but for its shape: every other feature in
Figure~\ref{fig:season-top6} rises, falls, and partially recovers within the sample window, while smart passes
falls steadily from 2011/12 to 2022/23 with no recovery at any point. A through-ball attempted at a
league-average rate in 2011/12 would be attempted well below that rate a decade later, holding player identity
fixed. Read as a tactical signal rather than a statistical curiosity, this is evidence of a leaguewide shift
away from risk-laden, line-breaking passing and toward lower-risk possession retention over the sample window,
consistent with the broader decline visible in the Chance creation and Passing quality panels of
Figure~\ref{fig:season-trends} (smart passes and through passes are themselves both Chance-creation
features).

Defensive engagement's split of the passing game is now visible directly in the feature groups themselves:
bulk, directional passing-volume features (forward, long, and lateral passes) load with its defensive pole,
while the Attacking passing group (key passes, smart passes, crosses, shot assists) loads with its attacking
pole, a split by purpose rather than by volume. Technical orientation is largely independent of it: a target
forward and a deep-lying passer can sit at a similar point on the defend/attack axis while landing at opposite
ends of this one. Possession retention does not match a simple wide-versus-direct-play description, hence its
``retention vs.\ crossing/defending'' label: its positive pole combines possession quality with direct goal
threat, and its negative pole mixes wide attacking play with contested defensive duels rather than sitting
cleanly on either side alone. It adds information the other two do not carry: two attacking players who agree
on both can still differ sharply in whether their output comes through retained possession and clean finishing
or through crossing into contested situations.

This section takes up, in depth, the question the Introduction posed at the outset: what does it mean for two
players to be ``similar,'' and can that be measured directly, from what the model has already learned about a
player's style, rather than asserted from reputation alone?

\subsection{Motivation: measuring style}

The model's own covariance structure already gives a principled way to define what ``similar'' means. Recall
(Definition~\ref{def:sigma_a}) that the player effect decomposes as
\[
\mathbf a_i = \boldsymbol\Lambda_a\boldsymbol\eta_i + \boldsymbol\psi_a \odot \mathbf z_{u,i},
\]
a \emph{shared} component driven by the $K^*=3$ canonical factors, and an \emph{idiosyncratic} component that
those factors do not explain. Style is read off the shared component: a player's position in the
$3$-dimensional canonical factor space, $\mathbf f_i$ (Definition~\ref{def:pca_postproc}), the same coordinates
used for the archetype clustering of \S\ref{sec:results-stylespace}, evaluated at its posterior mean throughout
this section.

Cristiano Ronaldo makes the point concretely. He is a genuine outlier in exactly the way his reputation
suggests: 5th most extreme player of 1{,}529 on Defensive engagement's attacking pole and 4th most extreme on
Possession retention's direct-goal-threat pole, the same company as Messi on both axes. His single closest style-neighbour under this
metric is Karim Benzema, a forward built the same way, direct and high-value in attack rather than a wide
creator or a deep passer. What that match does \emph{not} claim is that Benzema scored the way Ronaldo did over
this sample: nothing about how much either player scored enters $\mathbf f_i$, by construction. Matching
someone's shared style position is exactly what a nearest style-neighbour means; matching every idiosyncratic
thing that makes a generational player singular is a different, much harder claim this chapter does not make.
Whether a genuinely well-defined production score belongs alongside style similarity, and how to build one
that is not just a raw goals count, is future work (\S\ref{sec:results-future}).

\subsection{The similarity score}

Style distance, $d(i,j) = \lVert \bar{\mathbf f}_i - \bar{\mathbf f}_j \rVert$ (Euclidean, on canonical scores
standardised across all 1{,}529 outfield players), is the raw quantity behind every comparison in this
section: smaller means more similar. On its own, though, a raw distance is hard to read at a glance, $0.65$ or
$6.8$ mean little without a reference point, so this chapter also reports a normalised \textbf{similarity
score},
\[
\text{Similarity}(i,j) = 100 \times \exp\!\left(-\, d(i,j) / \bar d\right)\,\%,
\]
where $\bar d$ is the mean pairwise style distance across the whole population ($\bar d \approx 2.25$):
exactly $100\%$ only for an identical player ($d=0$), decaying smoothly as distance grows, calibrated so that
two players a population-typical distance apart score roughly $37\%$. The transform is monotone in $d(i,j)$,
so it reorders nothing, it only rescales the same distance onto an interpretable $0$--$100\%$ range.
Table~\ref{tab:similarity} and the forest-plot cards below report both numbers side by side.

\section{Applications: scouting and recommendations}
\label{sec:results-scouting}

Clubs field player requests through agents and recruitment platforms constantly, filtered on criteria the
model here says nothing about: position, age, contract status, market value. None of those filters says
anything about \emph{how} a player plays. Wanting ``a Xavi'' on a budget that will never buy the genuine
article is not an edge case, it is one of the most common requests a recruitment department makes, and
answering it well means exactly the separation this chapter builds: style, measured directly from playing
data, independent of reputation or price. Recruitment in practice is not a matter of sorting by a single
overall rating and taking the top name at a position; the useful question is almost always which of the
affordable options plays in the right \emph{way}.

The recipe follows directly from \S\ref{sec:results-similarity}: fix a target profile, take a candidate pool
already filtered on the hard, non-style criteria a club actually needs, rank what remains by similarity score,
and return a scored, ranked shortlist. A club will not get a player who performs at the target's level from
this alone, that half of the question is a genuine performance score, taken up as future work below, but it is
considerably more likely to find a player who \emph{plays like} the target than by working from reputation or
price tier alone. Being similar in style does not require being as good.

This is a style-matching tool, not a recruitment decision system: market value, injury history, contract
situation, and league context beyond what the model already conditions on are not modelled here and belong
alongside its output as separate filters, not replacements for it. Within that limit it offers something a
scout's individual judgement, applied one remembered name at a time, cannot: a precise, low-dimensional style
representation computed across an entire league at once. This kind of comparison already happens, by hand,
every transfer window; a tool that does it directly from data, at this level of precision and across a whole
population rather than a shortlist of remembered names, is not something readily available off the shelf.

\section{Future work}
\label{sec:results-future}

Four directions were considered during this work and are reported here as scoped future work rather than as
contributions of the present thesis.

\textbf{A performance score alongside similarity.} \S\ref{sec:results-similarity} deliberately measures style
only: two players can share a high similarity score while producing very differently, Stuani's case study
being the clearest example. A genuinely useful companion is a performance score, defined carefully enough to
survive scrutiny rather than a raw goals count (which conflates opportunity, minutes, and finishing quality)
or any single loose per90 rate. Paired with the similarity score, it would let a shortlist be built in two
steps instead of one: filter to players who play like the target, then rank that shortlist by this measure to
surface the best of them, rather than asking one number to do both jobs.

\textbf{Uncertainty beyond posterior means.} The model is fully Bayesian throughout, but two summaries still
reduce a full posterior to a single number: the archetype clustering of \S\ref{sec:results-stylespace} assigns
one hard $k$-means cluster per player rather than a soft, probability-weighted membership, and the similarity
scores of \S\ref{sec:results-similarity} report one distance per pair rather than a credible interval on it.
The first is a direct extension of the uncertainty already reported for canonical scores; the second is
harder, since a pairwise distance's uncertainty depends on the joint draw-by-draw behaviour of both players
together, not either one's marginal interval, and was not attempted here.

\textbf{Distance-based latent-space models.} Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}'s factor-analytic model represents
style through a shared covariance structure; a complementary alternative represents it directly through
pairwise dissimilarities via Bayesian multidimensional scaling \cite{oh2001bayesian}, with hierarchical
extensions for repeated player--season observations \cite{yanchenko2020hierarchical}. Chapter~\ref{chap:background}'s
classical MDS is a first, non-probabilistic step in that direction; a fully Bayesian version would attach
posterior uncertainty directly to each player's position rather than obtaining it indirectly through canonical
factor scores.

\textbf{Team-season effects and tactical fit.} No team identifier exists anywhere in the current data
pipeline, so a genuine player-vs-system comparison is not buildable without new data engineering. A
team-season effect $c_{t(i,j),j}$ would separate manager and tactical-system effects from stable player
identity and would be the natural foundation for a real tactical-fit score; the closest available proxy today
is simply a player's archetype cluster (\S\ref{sec:results-stylespace}).
